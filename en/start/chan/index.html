Chan Buddhism: From Bodhidharma to the Platform Sutra and the Lamp Records

Teachers, awakening, and the passing of a lamp

Begin with a teacher and a question

Chan Buddhism invites you to consider how awakening becomes evident in a person's life. Its literature places this question in encounters: a student arrives, asks for instruction, and receives a response fitted to the occasion. Sometimes the response is a sustained explanation; sometimes it is a question that changes the direction of the conversation. Reading slowly enough to hear both voices is a good beginning.

The word Chan comes from the Chinese rendering of Sanskrit dhyāna, meditation. The tradition developed in China and later became known elsewhere as Zen in Japan, Seon in Korea, and Thiền in Vietnam. Meditation, wisdom, and the relationship between teacher and student form recurring concerns. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's introduction to Chan provides a helpful account of these themes.

Two books give this guide its path: the Platform Sutra, which presents Huineng's teaching, and the Record of the Transmission of the Lamp, which arranges many teachers within a shared genealogy. Begin with the first, then let one name lead you into the second.

Bodhidharma and the six patriarchs

Tradition holds that Bodhidharma, a monk from India, transmitted the teaching to Huike in China. The familiar succession continues through Sengcan, Daoxin, Hongren, and Huineng. Together they are remembered as the six Chinese patriarchs. Scholars debate how the earliest stages of this lineage took shape in the surviving records.

Daoxin, who lived from 580 to 651, and Hongren, who lived from 601 to 674, bring the story to monastic communities at Huangmei. Hongren's community is associated with the East Mountain teaching. The Platform Sutra places both Shenxiu and Huineng among his students and makes the question of succession central to its opening narrative.

Keep a few place names alongside the personal names: Shaolin Temple; Sizu Temple, the Fourth Patriarch Temple; Wuzu Temple, the Fifth Patriarch Temple; and Nanhua Temple. These ancestral sites provide landmarks for the tradition's memory. For a first reading, a small notebook with a column for teachers and another for places is enough. Add a name only when it helps you follow the passage before you.

Meet Huineng in the Dunhuang Platform Sutra

Huineng lived from 638 to 713. The Platform Sutra presents him teaching at Dafan Temple in Shaozhou, addressing monks, nuns, officials, and laypeople. Its opening story recalls his life as a seller of firewood, his awakening upon hearing the Diamond Sutra, and his journey to study with Hongren. These episodes introduce the text's central conviction that wisdom can be realized regardless of a person's social standing.

The edition in this library is the Dunhuang version, T48n2007, a single fascicle. You can compare it with the CBETA record of T2007. This matters when using an English translation: chapter divisions and wording may follow a different recension. Match a passage by its opening words and subject as well as by any section number.

Read first the exchange in which Huineng tells Hongren that people have northern and southern origins, while buddha-nature has no such division. Then read the verse episode. Shenxiu describes sustained care for the mind through the image of polishing a mirror; Huineng's verses point to the original purity of buddha-nature. In this Dunhuang text, two verses are attributed to Huineng. Attend to the actual wording before bringing a familiar quotation from another edition to mind.

Meditation and wisdom, together

After the opening narrative, turn to the teaching on meditation and wisdom. Huineng compares their relationship to a lamp and its light: the two names identify inseparable aspects of one activity. The passage asks readers to consider how steadiness and understanding operate together. Read the analogy once, then return to the sentences immediately before it, where the teacher discusses the agreement of speech and conduct.

Three further expressions organize the teaching: no-thought, no-form, and non-abiding. The text explains no-thought through freedom from attachment amid seeing, hearing, and knowing. It explicitly warns against trying to eliminate every thought. Non-abiding concerns the mind's relationship to successive experiences: a thought arises, yet need not become a place in which one remains bound.

These passages become clearer beside the sutra's account of repentance, refuge, and the four great vows. Its audience is asked to acknowledge harmful conduct and undertake the work of wisdom and compassion. Try reading one doctrinal passage alongside one of these communal passages. Notice how the same vocabulary changes when a teacher is explaining an idea and when an assembly is voicing a commitment.

The five houses

Later Chan lineages are commonly grouped into five houses: Guiyang (溈仰), Linji (臨濟), Caodong (曹洞), Yunmen (雲門), and Fayan (法眼). Their teachers belong chiefly to the later Tang and Five Dynasties periods. The names help organize a growing field of communities and teaching records.

Guiyang is associated with Guishan Lingyou and Yangshan Huiji; Linji with Linji Yixuan; Caodong with Dongshan Liangjie and Caoshan Benji; Yunmen with Yunmen Wenyan; and Fayan with Fayan Wenyi. Keep these pairings as a reading aid. A house name may combine places associated with teachers or preserve the name by which a leading teacher became known.

You need not memorize the whole family tree before reading. Choose one teacher, read a short entry, and identify whom the teacher studied with and who comes to ask a question. Then compare a second encounter. This gives the house names a human scale and lets you hear differences between particular exchanges.

Reading the lamp records

The Record of the Transmission of the Lamp, or Jingde chuandeng lu, was compiled by Daoyuan and presented in 1004. It contains thirty fascicles and is numbered T2076 in the Taishō canon. Its preface describes the gathering of genealogies and recorded sayings, followed by editorial work under imperial commission. The CBETA edition of T2076 supplies a reference text.

Begin with fascicle three for Bodhidharma and the early Chinese patriarchs, then move to fascicle five for Huineng. Read the heading before the dialogue: it locates a teacher within a succession. Within the entry, distinguish biographical narrative, a student's question, the teacher's reply, and any verse. That simple separation makes a long collection much easier to navigate.

The image of passing a lamp expresses the continuation of awakening through teachers and students. The site's transmission chart offers another way to follow these relationships. Use it beside an entry, returning from the chart to the words of the encounter. A lineage gives you orientation; the conversation gives you something to read closely.

    A Chan collection of teachings attributed to Huineng, compiled in the name of Fahai.

  • Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp 景德傳燈錄

    Daoyuan's Song-dynasty compilation of Chan lineages and encounters.

Masters

  • Putidamo (Bodhidharma) 達摩

    Early Chinese sources associate Bodhidharma with meditation and teachings related to the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra.

  • Huike 慧可

    Huike is recognized in Chan tradition as the second Chinese patriarch.

  • Sengcan 僧璨

    Sengcan is recognized in Chan tradition as the third Chinese patriarch.

  • Daoxin 道信

    Daoxin led a monastic community at Mount Shuangfeng in Huangmei and taught Hongren.

  • Hongren 弘忍

    Hongren taught at the East Mountain community in Huangmei, following Daoxin.

  • Huineng 慧能

    Huineng (638–713) is remembered as a Chan teacher in southern China, associated with Caoxi and Baolin Monastery.
