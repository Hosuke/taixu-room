The Vinaya in China: Monastic Discipline and the Dharmaguptaka Rule

The texts that shape a community of practice

Begin with a community

A Buddhist monastery needs ways to receive new members, share resources, care for the sick, and settle disagreements. The Vinaya brings these everyday responsibilities into the path of Buddhist training. Its rules sit beside accounts of the occasions on which they were established, explanations of their scope, and procedures for acting together. Reading it brings you close to the practical life of the sangha, the community of ordained practitioners.

In Chinese, lü means monastic discipline, while jie usually refers to precepts. The familiar expression jielü brings the two together. A useful first question is therefore quite concrete: what kind of action is this passage describing? A rule for an individual, an admission ceremony, and a decision by an assembled community each ask for a different kind of attention.

The Four-Part Vinaya

The Four-Part Vinaya, or Sifen lü, preserves the discipline of the Indian Dharmaguptaka school. Buddhayaśas and Zhu Fonian worked on its Chinese translation in early fifth-century Chang’an. It is numbered T1428 in volume 22 of the Taishō canon and occupies sixty fascicles. The title refers to its four textual divisions; it does not mean that monastic life has only four rules.

The collection includes explanations of rules for monks and nuns, followed by substantial material on communal activities. Ordination, the rains retreat, robes, medicines, and disputes all have their place. The CBETA edition of T1428 provides a reference alongside the library’s text. On a first visit, choose one topic, such as receiving ordination or caring for someone who is ill, and follow a short sequence of cases.

In a rule’s explanation, notice the movement from an event to a decision. The text often names the people involved, describes a difficulty, and then states what the Buddha permits or prohibits. Further cases clarify how intention and circumstance affect its application. This structure makes the Vinaya a particularly rich source for studying how Buddhist communities understood responsibility.

A shorter doorway: the recitation text

For a manageable beginning, open the Dharmaguptaka Monks’ Precept Text, T1429. This is a prātimokṣa, a collection of rules used in the community’s regular recitation. The Dharmaguptaka reckoning gives fully ordained monks 250 rules. The full Vinaya supplies the narratives and explanations that surround these concise formulations.

Read the opening instructions before approaching the list. A speaker addresses the assembled monks, asks them to listen, and invites anyone conscious of an offence to acknowledge it. The text describes recitation twice each month. Its repeated questions about the community’s purity make responsibility something voiced and heard among other people.

The rules are grouped according to their character and seriousness. Four pārājika offences, conventionally translated as “defeats,” stand at the beginning; procedures for settling disputes appear at the end. Between them are several categories with different responses to wrongdoing. Keep those headings visible as you read. A short rule about etiquette and a rule involving loss of monastic standing belong to different parts of this carefully ordered collection.

Daoxuan on Mount Zhongnan

Daoxuan, who lived from 596 to 667, gave Chinese Vinaya learning an enduring shape. He studied under the Vinaya teacher Zhishou and worked in the region of Chang’an and Mount Zhongnan. The name Nanshan, “Southern Mountain,” connects the tradition associated with him to this mountain setting.

His Commentary on Practice, Abridging the Complexities and Supplementing the Omissions of the Four-Part Vinaya, T1804, is commonly called the Xingshi chao. The title conveys its practical purpose: to make a large body of disciplinary learning usable. Its opening contents include gathering the community, formal communal acts, ordination, relations between teachers and students, recitation, and the rains retreat.

Begin with the first section, “Setting Out the Guiding Principles and Revealing Their Virtue.” Daoxuan distinguishes the teaching of precepts, their reception within the practitioner, their continuing practice, and their outward expression. These distinctions offer a way to consider how a commitment becomes part of a life. Notice, too, his citations of Mahāyāna scriptures: he places discipline within the wider aspiration to awakening.

For an English account of the tradition’s later understanding of itself, BDK publishes Gyōnen’s Essentials of the Vinaya Tradition, translated by Leo M. Pruden. Its seventeenth question introduces Daoxuan, while its thirty-eighth question follows Vinaya learning in China. This is a later Japanese account, useful for seeing how his work was remembered and transmitted.

The bodhisattva precepts

The Brahmā’s Net Sutra, or Fanwang jing, T1484, introduces another important dimension of Chinese Buddhist ethical life. Its second fascicle sets out ten major and forty-eight minor bodhisattva precepts. It addresses a broad community, including monks, nuns, rulers, and lay followers, within the aspiration to buddhahood.

Tradition holds that Kumārajīva translated the scripture; modern scholarship generally places its composition in China in the fifth century. BDK’s English translation by A. Charles Muller and Kenneth K. Tanaka offers the complete two-fascicle text and an introduction to its history. In the Chinese library, turn first to the second fascicle and read the verses immediately before the major precepts.

These verses place receiving the precepts within confidence in the possibility of awakening. Then read the first major precept, concerning killing. It discusses causing or assisting harm as well as committing it oneself, and calls for compassion and the protection of living beings. The movement from restraint to active care is central to this passage.

The bodhisattva precepts and the Dharmaguptaka monastic code came to be used together in Chinese Buddhism. They have distinct textual structures and scopes: one speaks to bodhisattva commitments across a broad community, while the other organizes ordained communal life in detail. Following both helps explain why Chinese Buddhist discussions of discipline can move between admission to the sangha and concern for all beings.

Reading across the texts

Try placing three passages beside one another: the opening invitation to acknowledge offences in T1429, Daoxuan’s account of receiving and practising precepts, and the first major precept of the Brahmā’s Net Sutra. For each, note who is addressed, what commitment is being made, and what action follows. These small observations provide a firm beginning for a demanding body of literature.

You can then return to the longer Vinaya with a more focused question. How does a community respond when someone needs help? What makes a communal decision valid? How is a mistake acknowledged and addressed? The details become easier to follow when you read with the people involved in view.
