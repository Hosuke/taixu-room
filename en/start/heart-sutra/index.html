The Heart Sutra: Text, Translators and How to Read It

A short scripture with a long history of reading

A small doorway into a great tradition

The Heart Sutra brings the Buddhist teaching of wisdom into a remarkably small compass. Its full title, Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya, presents it as the “heart” of the Perfection of Wisdom. In a few paragraphs, it moves from the experience of a bodhisattva to the nature of things, freedom from fear, and a concluding mantra. You can read it in a few minutes and return to it throughout a lifetime of study.

Begin with the passage opening “Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva.” Our reading page includes prefatory material before the scripture, so this opening gives you a clear place to start. Read once for the movement of the whole: seeing, teaching, release, and invocation. On a second reading, pause at the recurring words “form,” “emptiness,” and “wisdom.” Their relationships will become clearer as you meet them again.

Xuanzang and the Chinese text

This guide follows the Chinese version attributed to Xuanzang, the seventh-century monk whose journeys and translation work helped shape Chinese Buddhism. In the Taishō Buddhist canon it is text number 251, usually abbreviated T251, in volume 8. That number is a useful companion when comparing editions, translations, and commentaries. The CBETA edition supplies the text and its textual notes.

The Heart Sutra belongs to a much larger family of Perfection of Wisdom scriptures, represented in this library by the Large Perfection of Wisdom Sutra. These works explore how a bodhisattva develops wisdom while working for the liberation of living beings. Scholars debate the Heart Sutra's formation and the relationship between its Chinese and Sanskrit versions. For this first reading, Xuanzang's concise Chinese version provides a clear, continuous text to follow.

Avalokiteśvara and the five aggregates

The sutra opens with Avalokiteśvara, the bodhisattva known in Chinese as Guanyin and named Guanzizai in this text. While practicing profound Perfection of Wisdom, Avalokiteśvara sees that the five aggregates are empty and passes beyond suffering. The scene joins insight with compassion and liberation from its very first sentence.

The five aggregates are form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. They offer a way of examining embodied experience: the body, the pleasant or unpleasant quality of experience, recognition, the activities that shape a response, and awareness. Chinese readers meet them as 色、受、想、行、識. Keep this short list nearby; the sutra will return to it almost immediately.

A helpful first exercise is to take one ordinary experience, such as hearing a bell. There is a bodily encounter with sound, a feeling about it, recognition, a response, and awareness. This exercise gives the vocabulary something familiar to work with. Then return to Avalokiteśvara's insight: what changes when these aspects of experience are seen as empty of an independent, fixed nature?

Reading form and emptiness together

Avalokiteśvara addresses Śāriputra, called Shelizi in the Chinese text. The teaching unfolds through four closely related statements: form does not differ from emptiness; emptiness does not differ from form; form is emptiness; emptiness is form. The remaining four aggregates are then included in the same understanding.

In this teaching, emptiness concerns the absence of an independent nature in things. Form and emptiness belong together: the very experience before us is what wisdom examines. Read the four statements slowly, allowing the return from emptiness to form to carry as much weight as the movement from form to emptiness. The paired sentences are an excellent place to begin reading with a commentary.

Next comes a sequence of three pairs: neither arising nor ceasing, neither defiled nor pure, neither increasing nor decreasing. Notice the balanced rhythm. The text loosens the habit of treating either side of each pair as something finally graspable. Its language invites a patient attention that can stay with a question longer than the first answer.

Finding your way through the many instances of “no”

The central passage gathers several Buddhist teaching lists. It moves through the five aggregates, the six sense faculties and their objects, the field of sensory consciousness, dependent arising, and the four noble truths. The Chinese compresses some lists with “up to” or “and so on,” giving their first and last members. Mark these groups as you read; a dense paragraph soon becomes easier to navigate.

For the senses, begin with the familiar eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body, then include mind as the sixth faculty. For the four truths, identify suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. The passage brings even the teachings used to understand suffering within its examination of emptiness. Finally, wisdom and attainment enter the same sequence.

Read this movement alongside the next paragraph. After speaking of non-attainment, the sutra describes bodhisattvas relying on Perfection of Wisdom, becoming free from mental obstruction and fear, and reaching nirvāṇa. It then speaks of the buddhas of past, present, and future attaining complete awakening through this wisdom. Hold these sentences together: the release of grasping and the fulfillment of the path illuminate one another.

The mantra in Sanskrit and Chinese

The final passage praises Perfection of Wisdom and gives its mantra. Here are its Sanskrit sounds alongside the Chinese transcription in the Taishō printing of Xuanzang's version:

Sanskrit romanization Chinese transcription
gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā 揭帝揭帝 般羅揭帝 般羅僧揭帝 菩提僧莎訶

The text's apparatus records several spellings: CBETA's reading text adopts 莎婆訶 for the final group, while the Taishō printing has 僧莎訶. The Chinese characters here carry sound. The Sanskrit evokes going beyond and awakening, bringing the sutra's teaching into a memorable spoken cadence. Copying the two lines together is a useful way to become familiar with their correspondence.

Read the paragraph before the mantra as well. Its repeated praise joins the closing sounds to everything that precedes them: wisdom, freedom from fear, and the passage beyond suffering. The ending gathers the whole scripture into an act of recitation.

Learning with the commentators

Kuiji, a disciple of Xuanzang, composed an early commentary catalogued as T1710. The BDK English translation offers a detailed companion, explaining the scripture through the learning of the Faxiang and Sanlun traditions. A manageable beginning is to read its discussion of the title and then its explanation of form and emptiness.

The library also offers commentaries by Hanshan Deqing, Kūkai, and Taixu. Choose one sentence from the sutra and spend a session with one commentator. Over time, comparing their explanations will introduce the distinct voices through which this short scripture has been taught.

Masters

  • Xuanzang 玄奘

    Xuanzang (602–664) travelled through Central Asia to India to resolve questions about Buddhist doctrine.

  • Kuiji 窺基

    Kuiji (632–682) studied under Xuanzang and worked in his translation circle in Chang'an.

  • Hanshan Deqing 德清

    Hanshan Deqing wrote commentaries on the Diamond, Śūraṅgama, Laṅkāvatāra and other sūtras while advocating both Chan and Pure Land practice.

  • Kūkai 空海

    Kūkai (774–835), born in Sanuki, travelled to Tang China in 804.

  • Taixu 太虛

    Taixu advocated reforms of Buddhist institutions, monastic education and religious practice in modern China.
