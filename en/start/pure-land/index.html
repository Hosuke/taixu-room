Pure Land Buddhism: Amitābha, the Three Sutras and the Practice of Recollection

A Buddha's vows and a name held in mind

Begin with the Buddha's name

Pure Land Buddhism centres on Amitābha Buddha and the aspiration for rebirth in his land, Sukhāvatī, where the conditions for pursuing awakening are fulfilled. In Chinese, this land is called Jile, Utmost Bliss. Amitābha means immeasurable light; the related name Amitāyus means immeasurable life. Chinese scriptures use Amituo fo, a name you will meet throughout the texts in this library.

A good first question is what the scriptures ask their listeners to remember. They describe the Buddha's vows, the qualities of his land, and the lives of people who turn towards him. The practice called nianfo, recollection of the Buddha, can include contemplation of the Buddha and vocal recitation of his name. Reading with both senses in mind helps you follow passages that move between vision, attention, and speech.

The three principal sutras offer complementary beginnings. The shorter sutra introduces the land and the name; the larger tells the story of the vows; the contemplation sutra begins with a person in distress and unfolds a path of contemplation and rebirth.

Start with the shorter sutra

The Amitābha Sutra, T366, is the shortest of the three. Kumārajīva's Chinese translation opens with Śākyamuni addressing Śāriputra. Its description of Sukhāvatī includes lotus ponds, jewelled trees, and birds whose calls communicate Buddhist teachings. Read the description once as a scene, then again for what each part of the scene enables its inhabitants to hear and remember.

The birds provide a particularly useful detail. The sutra explains that Amitābha manifests them to proclaim the Dharma. Their voices lead listeners to recollect the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Beauty here has a teaching function: sounds and surroundings support the direction of a person's attention.

Next find the passage on holding the Buddha's name in mind for one to seven days with undistracted attention. Read it together with the repeated encouragement to aspire to birth in that land. This gives you three threads to keep together as you continue: hearing of Amitābha, holding his name, and forming an aspiration. The scripture's brief compass makes it a welcoming text to revisit.

The larger sutra and the vows

The Infinite Life Sutra, T360, tells of the monk Dharmākara, who learns from the Buddha Lokeśvararāja and makes vows concerning the land and community he will bring into being. In this Chinese version there are forty-eight vows. Their fulfilment is presented as Amitābha's attainment of buddhahood.

Begin with the vows concerning the light and life of the Buddha, then turn to the eighteenth vow. It concerns beings who sincerely entrust themselves, aspire to birth in his land, and recollect him even ten times. Read its final qualification as well: the text names the five grave offences and slander of the true Dharma. Later commentators carefully consider this wording alongside the contemplation sutra's account of people burdened by serious wrongdoing.

Then read the nineteenth and twentieth vows, noticing awakening, virtuous activity, aspiration, and dedication. This small group offers a manageable entrance into a long scripture. As you read further, mark what the vows promise about the possibility of learning and continuing on the bodhisattva path. The land's splendour and its religious purpose belong together.

The BDK translation of the three Pure Land sutras, by Hisao Inagaki with Harold Stewart, offers an English companion to T360, T365, and T366. Its titles use Amitāyus, so the names may look different while the three Taishō numbers remain a clear guide.

A queen's question and sixteen contemplations

The Contemplation Sutra, T365, begins with Queen Vaidehī's suffering amid the violence of her son Ajātaśatru. She asks Śākyamuni to show her a land free from such distress and chooses Amitābha's land. The teaching proceeds through sixteen contemplations, beginning with the setting sun and moving through the land's features, the Buddha, and the attendant bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta.

Before following the visual details, read the passage on three kinds of meritorious conduct. It includes care for parents and teachers, refraining from killing, ethical discipline, and the aspiration to awakening. This places the contemplation within the life and conduct of the person who undertakes it.

The final three contemplations describe nine grades of rebirth. Near the end, a person approaching death, overwhelmed by suffering and serious past wrongdoing, receives help from a good spiritual friend and is instructed to voice the Buddha's name. Notice the companion's role: teaching is offered in a form the person can receive at that moment. Compare this scene with the larger sutra's eighteenth vow, keeping both passages open before turning to a commentary.

Huiyuan and the memory of Mount Lu

Huiyuan, who lived from 334 to 416, established a community at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu. Tradition holds that in 402 he gathered monks and lay followers there to recollect Amitābha and vow birth in the western land. Later Pure Land tradition honours him as an early patriarch and remembers the community through the image of the White Lotus Society.

Huiyuan also exchanged letters with Kumārajīva about Buddhist doctrine, including questions concerning the Buddha encountered in contemplation. Scholars debate how closely Huiyuan's own understanding corresponds to later Pure Land teaching. His place in this story invites attention to the relationship between shared practice, the wish to encounter a Buddha, and the questions that arise through meditation.

Tanluan, Daochuo, and Shandao

Tanluan, traditionally dated 476–542, wrote a commentary on the Treatise on Rebirth attributed to Vasubandhu. His discussion makes Amitābha's vow-power central to progress on the path and gives dedication of merit an important place. In the CBETA text of his commentary, T1819, begin with the opening comparison of difficult and easy practice, then read the closing discussion of the Buddha's vows.

Daochuo, 562–645, taught at Xuanzhong Monastery. His Collection on Peace and Bliss distinguishes the path of sages from the Pure Land path, asking how people in his own time can attain liberation. His teaching connects the interpretation of scripture with the circumstances of those who seek instruction.

Shandao, 613–681, studied with Daochuo and later taught in Chang'an. His Commentary on the Contemplation Sutra, T1753, explains the scripture in four fascicles. In the fourth, find the passage distinguishing principal and supporting practices. Shandao places sustained recitation of Amitābha's name at the centre because it accords with the Buddha's vow.

Read this passage alongside the famous image of a narrow white path between rivers of water and fire, also in the fourth fascicle. The traveller hears encouragement to proceed and an invitation from the farther shore. Shandao interprets the image through the practitioner's troubled mind, trust, and aspiration. Returning from the image to the sutras helps you see how commentary gives memorable form to their promises and instructions.

Where to go next

  • The Amitābha Sutra: read the description of the land and the passage on holding the name.
  • The Infinite Life Sutra: begin with Dharmākara's vows, especially the eighteenth to twentieth.
  • The Contemplation Sutra: follow Vaidehī's question and the closing accounts of rebirth.
  • Shandao's commentary: explore the practices and the parable of the white path in fascicle four.
  • Chan Buddhism: meet another major current of Chinese Buddhist teaching through its teachers and texts.
