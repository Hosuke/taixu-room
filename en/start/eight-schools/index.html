The Eight Schools of Chinese Buddhism

Eight routes through a shared library

Eight invitations to learn

Chinese Buddhism offers many ways to approach the same large concerns: suffering, wisdom, compassion, and the possibility of awakening. The eight-school map gathers these approaches under familiar names. Some emphasize philosophical reasoning, others meditation, devotion, discipline, or ritual. Teachers and communities have often drawn on several of them together. You can use the map to find a starting point and then follow the connections that interest you.

For each school below, meet a teacher, open a characteristic text, and try one small reading exercise. The aim is to hear each tradition's own questions clearly. A passage that seems difficult on the first encounter may become illuminating after you have spent time with another school's concerns.

Sanlun: learning through careful questioning

Sanlun means “Three Treatises”: the Middle Treatise, T1564; the Hundred Treatise, T1569; and the Twelve Gate Treatise, T1568. Kumārajīva translated this literature in the early fifth century. Jizang, who lived from 549 to 623, became one of its leading Chinese interpreters. The tradition develops Madhyamaka reasoning about emptiness: things arise through conditions and lack an independent nature of their own.

Start with the Middle Treatise's first chapter, on conditions. Follow one question and its answer slowly, identifying the account of arising being examined. Then turn to chapter 24, on the four noble truths, where emptiness and dependent arising are brought together. Reading the two chapters as a pair helps you see how critical reasoning serves the Buddhist path.

Consciousness-Only: understanding experience

The Chinese Yogācāra tradition is commonly called Weishi, “Consciousness-Only,” or Faxiang, “Dharma Characteristics.” Xuanzang and his disciple Kuiji were central seventh-century teachers. Their scholarly world included Great Ci'en Temple in Chang'an. The Discourse on the Establishment of Consciousness-Only, T1585, draws together interpretations of Vasubandhu's Thirty Verses. BDK offers an English translation in Three Texts on Consciousness Only.

Begin with the opening explanation of why the treatise was written: understanding the grasping at self and phenomena, removing obstacles, and attaining liberation and awakening. This gives the later technical vocabulary a purpose. As you read, keep a small list of terms for consciousness and mental activity, adding the example or distinction that helps you understand each one.

Tiantai: bringing teaching and contemplation together

Zhiyi, 538–597, gave Tiantai its formative system of scriptural interpretation and contemplative practice. The school takes its name from Mount Tiantai in Zhejiang. Its central scripture is the Lotus Sutra, and Zhiyi's teachings on calming and insight are preserved in works including the Great Calming and Contemplation, T1911. Scripture and meditation illuminate each other throughout this tradition.

Read the Lotus Sutra's chapter 5, the parable of medicinal plants. Rain falls from one cloud, while plants receive nourishment according to their capacities. Ask how this image relates a common teaching to the differences among learners. Then return to chapter 2 and its discussion of skillful means. These passages prepare you for Tiantai's patient attention to both the unity and variety of Buddhist teaching.

Huayan: discovering relationships

Huayan takes its name from the Flower Garland Sutra, or Avataṃsaka. Fazang, 643–712, was a major interpreter during the Tang dynasty. Huayan examines how each phenomenon is understood through its relations with others. Its vision joins detailed philosophical inquiry to an expansive world of bodhisattvas, vows, and journeys toward awakening. The Stanford Encyclopedia's Huayan entry provides further context.

For a compact first reading, open Fazang's Essay on the Golden Lion, T1881, transmitted here with Chengqian's commentary. Begin with its account of a craftsman making a lion from gold. Follow how material, form, and conditions enter the explanation; then consider how the image helps Fazang describe the relation of parts and whole. Carry that question into the larger scripture.

Chan: awakening in the midst of life

Chan places awakening and the relationship between teacher and student at the center of its literature. Tradition honors Huineng, 638–713, as its Sixth Patriarch. The Platform Sutra, T2008, presents his teaching through a life story, sermons, and exchanges with students. Its opening narrative connects his awakening with hearing the Diamond Sutra and his subsequent journey to study with Hongren.

Start with that opening narrative, then read the section on meditation and wisdom. Notice the lamp-and-light image used to describe their relationship. In the dialogues, keep the question beside the reply: the encounter often becomes clearer when you attend to what the student is asking. This is an inviting way to appreciate Chan's combination of direct speech, memorable imagery, and close attention to learning.

Pure Land: vows and remembrance

Pure Land Buddhism centers on Amitābha and rebirth in his land, Sukhāvatī. Its principal scriptures include the Larger Sutra, T360, and the Amitābha Sutra, T366. Huiyuan, 334–416, is associated with Mount Lu, while Shandao, 613–681, became a major interpreter of Pure Land scripture and practice during the Tang. Their writings belong to a long tradition of aspiration, remembrance, and devotion.

Begin with the shorter Amitābha Sutra's description of Sukhāvatī. Then read the vows of Dharmākara, the future Amitābha, in the Larger Sutra. Notice what kinds of suffering those vows address and what opportunities for learning they promise. The relationship between a buddha's compassion and a practitioner's aspiration gives these passages their warmth and direction.

Vinaya: caring for a community

Vinaya concerns the conduct, ordination, and shared procedures of monastic life. Daoxuan, 596–667, was a leading Chinese interpreter of the Four-Part Vinaya, T1428, the disciplinary collection of the Dharmaguptaka tradition. His work helped organize the study and application of its rules. The subjects are concrete: clothing, food, possessions, relationships, and the decisions a community makes together.

Choose one rule and read its introductory narrative before its formal statement. Notice who raises the problem and how the response is explained. Then follow any distinctions or exceptions. This close reading reveals the practical care involved in maintaining a community, and gives ethical vocabulary a setting in everyday actions.

Esoteric Buddhism: body, speech, and mind

Tang esoteric Buddhism flourished through translators and teachers including Śubhakarasiṃha, Vajrabodhi, and Amoghavajra. Its major scriptures include the Mahāvairocana Sutra, T848, and the Vajraśekhara Sutra, T865. Mantra, hand gestures, visualization, and mandalas coordinate speech, body, and mind in a path guided by a teacher.

Begin with the opening of the Mahāvairocana Sutra, where Vajrapāṇi asks about awakening. Attend to the answer's connection between the awakening mind, compassion, and skillful means. This gives a clear orientation before the detailed ritual sections. Our Tang esoteric guide follows the people and places that helped this literature travel through Chang'an and onward to Japan.

Texts to read

  • Middle Treatise 中論

    Nāgārjuna's verses on emptiness and dependent arising, with commentary attributed to Qingmu.

  • Hundred Treatise 百論

    A Madhyamaka treatise attributed to Āryadeva with commentary attributed to Vasu.

  • Treatise on the Twelve Gates 十二門論

    A Madhyamaka work attributed to Nāgārjuna examining emptiness through twelve lines of argument.

  • Demonstration of Consciousness Only 成唯識論

    A Yogācāra synthesis drawing on Indian commentaries to Vasubandhu's Thirty Verses.

  • Lotus Sutra 妙法蓮華經

    A Mahayana scripture on the one vehicle, skillful means, and the possibility of buddhahood.

  • Flower Ornament Sutra 大方廣佛華嚴經

    The eighty-fascicle Chinese Avataṃsaka collection, important to Huayan Buddhism.

  • Treatise on the Golden Lion 華嚴金師子章

    Fazang's explanation of Huayan interdependence through the image of a golden lion.

  • Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch 六祖壇經

    A Chan collection of teachings attributed to Huineng, compiled in the name of Fahai.

  • Amitābha Sutra 阿彌陀經

    The shorter Pure Land scripture on Sukhāvatī and recollection of Amitābha.

  • Larger Sutra of Immeasurable Life 無量壽經

    A Pure Land scripture on Amitābha's vows and the land of Sukhāvatī.

  • Four-Part Vinaya 四分律

    The Dharmaguptaka collection of monastic rules and accounts of their establishment.

  • Mahāvairocana Sutra 大日經

    An esoteric scripture on awakening, mantra, and maṇḍala practice, central to Tang Esoteric Buddhism and Shingon.

  • Vajraśekhara Sutra 金剛頂經

    An esoteric scripture on Vajradhātu maṇḍala practice and the realization of buddhahood.

  • My Approach to Classifying Buddhist Teachings 我怎樣判攝一切佛法

    Taixu explains his approach to classifying the full range of Buddhist teachings.

Masters

  • Jizang 吉藏

    Jizang (549–623) studied under Falang at Xinghuang Monastery in Jiankang.

  • Xuanzang 玄奘

    Xuanzang (602–664) travelled through Central Asia to India to resolve questions about Buddhist doctrine.

  • Kuiji 窺基

    Kuiji (632–682) studied under Xuanzang and worked in his translation circle in Chang'an.

  • Zhiyi 智顗

    Zhiyi (538–597) studied under Huisi before teaching in the southern capital and at Mount Tiantai.

  • Fazang 法藏

    Fazang (643–712) was born in Chang'an to a family of Sogdian ancestry and studied under Zhiyan.

  • Huineng 慧能

    Huineng (638–713) is remembered as a Chan teacher in southern China, associated with Caoxi and Baolin Monastery.

  • Lushan Huiyuan 慧遠

    Huiyuan studied under Dao'an before establishing a monastic community at Donglin Monastery on Mount Lu.

  • Shandao 善導

    Shandao (613–681) studied Pure Land practice under Daochuo at Xuanzhong Monastery before teaching in Chang'an.

  • Daoxuan 道宣

    Daoxuan (596–667) studied monastic discipline under Zhishou and spent much of his career around Chang'an and Mount Zhongnan.

  • Bukong (Amoghavajra) 不空

    Born in 705, Amoghavajra became Vajrabodhi's disciple while young and accompanied him to Tang China.

  • Taixu 太虛

    Taixu advocated reforms of Buddhist institutions, monastic education and religious practice in modern China.
