Mind-Only and the Middle Way: Yogācāra and Madhyamaka in China

Emptiness, consciousness, and the work of understanding

Begin with a question about experience

A thought arises, catches your attention, and shapes what you do next. Buddhist philosophers ask how this happens, what makes the thought seem to belong to a self, and how understanding the process can relieve suffering. Madhyamaka and Yogācāra developed different ways of pursuing these questions. In China, their histories can be followed through translators, commentaries, and a few short texts that reward patient reading.

Madhyamaka means the Middle Way. Its Chinese Three Treatises tradition is called Sanlun. Yogācāra became especially influential through the learning associated with Xuanzang and Kuiji, often called Weishi, “consciousness-only,” or Faxiang, the tradition concerned with the characteristics of dharmas. A dharma in this setting can mean a phenomenon or an element used in analysing experience. Keeping that meaning in view will help when you encounter lists of them.

Kumārajīva and the Three Treatises

Kumārajīva arrived in Chang’an in 401. With the support of the Later Qin ruler Yao Xing and the work of Chinese collaborators, his translations gave Buddhist readers new access to Indian philosophical argument. Three works became the textual foundation of Sanlun: the Middle Treatise, T1564; the Twelve Gate Treatise, T1568; and the Hundred Treatise, T1569.

The Middle Treatise presents verses attributed to Nāgārjuna with a commentary attributed to Qingmu, whose name is conventionally rendered as Piṅgala. The Twelve Gate Treatise is traditionally attributed to Nāgārjuna; the Hundred Treatise presents verses attributed to Āryadeva with commentary attributed to Vasu. Their Chinese headings preserve the names of authors, commentators, and translator. Read these headings first: they show how several voices can meet in a single work.

For a first substantial passage, turn to chapter 24 of the Middle Treatise, on the Four Noble Truths. An objection claims that emptiness would undermine suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. The reply explains why dependent arising and emptiness allow these teachings to make sense. Something with a fixed, independent nature could neither arise through conditions nor change when those conditions change.

Read the objection and reply as a pair. Mark each change of speaker, then follow the example of suffering: what would it mean for suffering to possess an unalterable nature? The argument makes emptiness a way of understanding the possibility of transformation. The CBETA edition of T1564 supplies a reference text for comparing the Chinese.

Jizang and the use of the two truths

Jizang, 549–623, became a leading interpreter of the Three Treatises. He studied under Falang and later taught at Jiaxiang Monastery in Kuaiji and in Chang’an. His work on the two truths asks how language can guide understanding without becoming an object of attachment.

Conventional truth concerns the ways we speak and understand within shared experience. Ultimate truth concerns the emptiness of the things to which those expressions refer. Jizang treats these as ways of teaching. A statement can loosen one attachment, then itself require clarification when a reader begins to cling to it. This gives his writing a conversational movement: a claim meets an objection, and the reply changes the standpoint from which the question was asked.

Keep a small reading notebook with three columns: the claim, the assumption behind it, and the reply. Begin with the Middle Treatise passage above, then use Jizang’s profile to orient yourself among his writings. The exercise encourages attention to the work an argument performs, as well as to the conclusion it reaches.

Xuanzang and Kuiji in Chang’an

Xuanzang, 602–664, travelled through Central Asia to India and studied at Nālandā under Śīlabhadra. He returned to Chang’an in 645 with Sanskrit manuscripts and directed major translation projects. Kuiji, 632–682, studied with him and became closely associated with Daci’en Monastery. His commentaries helped establish a durable Chinese tradition of Yogācāra learning.

The Demonstration of Consciousness Only, or Cheng weishi lun, T1585, is a ten-fascicle synthesis based on Indian commentaries on Vasubandhu’s Thirty Verses. It gives particular weight to Dharmapāla’s interpretation. Kuiji’s commentary, T1830, explains its arguments and terminology. Francis H. Cook’s Three Texts on Consciousness Only offers an English translation of T1585 together with two shorter works.

You can begin with the short root text before approaching these larger commentaries. Read one verse, identify its main terms, and then consult the fuller explanation. This keeps the architecture of the argument visible while allowing its details to accumulate gradually.

Read the Thirty Verses in three movements

The library’s Thirty Verses on Consciousness Only, T1586, is Xuanzang’s Chinese translation of Vasubandhu’s work. Its accompanying explanations distinguish the characteristics of consciousness, its true nature, and the stages of practice. Notice how the final verses bring the analysis back to the path of awakening.

First follow the account of consciousness. The text distinguishes the store consciousness, ālayavijñāna; the self-oriented activity of manas; and the six consciousnesses associated with sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, and mental cognition. The store consciousness carries seeds that mature through conditions. The text compares its continuity to a rushing stream. Read that image alongside the later account of habits and karmic continuity.

Next read verses 20–25 on the three natures. The imagined nature concerns what conceptual construction projects; the dependent nature concerns experience arising through conditions; the perfected nature concerns the absence of the imagined in the dependent. Take your time with their relationship. Scholars debate how best to describe Yogācāra’s philosophical position in modern terms, including whether “idealism” captures it adequately; the Stanford Encyclopedia’s survey explains the main interpretations.

Finally read verses 26–30 on practice and realization. Verse 27 is especially helpful: treating consciousness-only as a little object placed before the mind still leaves something grasped. Read it again after the earlier analysis of consciousness. The text’s own movement carries the reader from explanation towards the transformation of grasping.

Use the hundred dharmas as a reading aid

The Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas, T1614, is a compact work traditionally attributed to Vasubandhu and translated by Xuanzang. It groups its hundred items into eight consciousnesses, fifty-one mental factors, eleven material phenomena, twenty-four factors dissociated from mind, and six unconditioned dharmas.

Start with the five mental factors described as universally present: attention, contact, feeling, perception, and volition. Compare them with the more familiar terms among the wholesome factors, such as confidence, diligence, and non-harming. Make a few notes beside a passage of the Thirty Verses whenever one of these terms appears. The list then becomes a usable vocabulary.

The treatise opens by asking what is meant by all dharmas and by no-self; it closes by distinguishing the selflessness of persons and of dharmas. Read its beginning and ending together before studying the full list. They place its exact counting within a question shared by the texts throughout this guide: how can careful understanding loosen the hold of an imagined self?

Where to go next

  • Middle Treatise: read chapter 24 with its objections and replies together.
  • Jizang: follow the Chinese interpretation of the Three Treatises.
  • Thirty Verses on Consciousness Only: begin with the three transformations of consciousness.
  • Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas: learn the five groups and the opening question.
  • Tiantai and Huayan: explore further Chinese approaches to doctrine and contemplation.

Texts to read

  • Middle Treatise 中論

    Nāgārjuna's verses on emptiness and dependent arising, with commentary attributed to Qingmu.

  • Treatise on the Twelve Gates 十二門論

    A Madhyamaka work attributed to Nāgārjuna examining emptiness through twelve lines of argument.

  • Hundred Treatise 百論

    A Madhyamaka treatise attributed to Āryadeva with commentary attributed to Vasu.

  • Demonstration of Consciousness Only 成唯識論

    A Yogācāra synthesis drawing on Indian commentaries to Vasubandhu's Thirty Verses.

  • Commentary on the Demonstration of Consciousness Only 成唯識論述記

    Kuiji's detailed commentary on Xuanzang's Yogācāra synthesis.

  • Thirty Verses on Consciousness Only 唯識三十論頌

    Vasubandhu's concise verse presentation of Yogācāra philosophy.

  • Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas 大乘百法明門論

    A Yogācāra classification of one hundred phenomena attributed to Vasubandhu.

Masters

  • Jiumoluoshi (Kumārajīva) 鳩摩羅什

    Kumārajīva (344–413) grew up in Kucha and studied Buddhist teachings in Central Asia and Kashmir.

  • Jizang 吉藏

    Jizang (549–623) studied under Falang at Xinghuang Monastery in Jiankang.

  • Xuanzang 玄奘

    Xuanzang (602–664) travelled through Central Asia to India to resolve questions about Buddhist doctrine.

  • Kuiji 窺基

    Kuiji (632–682) studied under Xuanzang and worked in his translation circle in Chang'an.
