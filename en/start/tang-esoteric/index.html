Esoteric Buddhism in Tang Chang'an: Amoghavajra, Huiguo and the Road to Kūkai

Teachers, scriptures, and a journey across the sea

Begin in Chang’an

In eighth-century Chang’an, the Tang capital at present-day Xi’an, Buddhist teachers worked across languages and brought texts, images, and ritual practices into conversation. Two monasteries give this history a clear shape: Daxingshan, closely associated with Amoghavajra, and Qinglong, where Huiguo taught Kūkai. Following their teachers and books offers a welcoming route into Tang esoteric Buddhism.

The tradition brings body, speech, and mind together in the path to awakening. A mudrā is a ritual gesture; a mantra is a sacred verbal formula; a mandala presents an ordered assembly of buddhas and bodhisattvas. Initiation, called abhiṣeka, admits a student to a particular teaching and practice. These terms become easier to understand when you meet them in a passage with a teacher, an assembly, and a stated purpose.

The teachers and their work

Śubhakarasiṃha, Vajrabodhi, and Amoghavajra are traditionally known as the three great esoteric masters of the Kaiyuan era, the reign period from 713 to 741. Śubhakarasiṃha and the Chinese monk Yixing worked on the Chinese Mahāvairocana Sutra, numbered T848 in the Taishō canon. Vajrabodhi and his disciple Amoghavajra transmitted teachings associated with the Vajraśekhara, or Diamond Peak, scriptures.

Amoghavajra was born in 705 and became Vajrabodhi’s disciple while young. After his teacher’s death, he travelled to Sri Lanka, known in Chinese sources as the Lion Kingdom, to seek further teachings and texts. He returned to China and eventually made Daxingshan Temple in Chang’an a principal centre of his work. His career extended through the reigns of Xuanzong, Suzong, and Daizong; he died in 774.

His activity joined translation, teaching, and ritual service. Imperial support brought resources to these undertakings, while scriptures concerned with protection linked Buddhist learning to the responsibilities of government. Read a translation’s opening attribution as a first introduction to this world: the translator’s name, his temple, and the mention of an imperial commission can place a short text within a much larger network of people.

Start with wisdom and compassion

A good first passage is the opening chapter of the Mahāvairocana Sutra. In a dialogue with Vajrapāṇi, the Buddha presents the mind of awakening as the cause, compassion as the root, and skilful means as the culmination. These three terms give you a foundation for the detailed ritual instructions that follow. Pause over the relationship between understanding, care for others, and the ability to act helpfully.

Next read the Sūtra of the Guiding Principle (Liqu jing), T243, known in Japanese as the Rishukyō. Amoghavajra’s translation places Perfection of Wisdom teaching in an esoteric setting. Begin with the opening series of statements about purity, then follow the explanation that the nature of all phenomena is pure. Notice how the scripture gathers sight, sound, feeling, and other aspects of experience into its teaching.

As you continue, observe the recurring pattern: a teaching is given, a figure expresses it through a gesture, and a syllable or mantra follows. This helps you read the text as a coordinated whole. For extended study, Ian Astley-Kristensen’s The Rishukyō (1991) provides an English translation and detailed annotations.

Explore the range of Amoghavajra’s translations

The Humane Kings Scripture for the Protection of the State, T246, connects wisdom with the welfare of a kingdom. Turn first to its fifth chapter, on protecting the state, and notice the place given to receiving and explaining the teaching. Then return to the opening imperial preface. Together, these passages show why a Buddhist scripture could matter both to a religious community and to a ruler.

Amoghavajra’s Vajraśekhara Sutra, T865, opens a more elaborate world of awakening and mandala assemblies. The CBETA text of T865 offers a reliable reference for this scripture. Begin with its opening assembly and follow the central figures before trying to absorb every ritual detail. The repeated names and actions gradually make the structure familiar.

For a shorter reading, try the Praise of Samantabhadra’s Vows, T297. Its verses move through homage, offerings, acknowledgment of wrongdoing, rejoicing, requests for teaching, and dedication. Read the opening sequence aloud and notice how the speaker’s concern widens towards all beings. It offers a warm point of contact between esoteric learning and the broader Mahāyāna aspiration to awakening.

The Cundī dhāraṇī scripture, T1076, and the Mārīcī scripture, T1255b, introduce protective recitation. In the latter, begin with the opening account of Mārīcī, then read the prayer naming dangers on journeys and in daily life. According to the scripture, remembering her and reciting the dhāraṇī bring protection. Attend to the hopes and anxieties voiced by the prayer as well as its ritual form.

Huiguo and Kūkai at Qinglong Temple

Huiguo, a disciple of Amoghavajra, taught at Qinglong Temple in Chang’an. Kūkai travelled from Japan to Tang China in 804. In 805 he studied with Huiguo and received the teachings and initiations of both the Womb Realm and the Diamond Realm mandalas. Huiguo died at the end of 805, and Kūkai returned to Japan in 806. This transmission became a foundation of Japanese Shingon Buddhism.

Kūkai’s Catalogue of Imported Items, submitted in 806, is a particularly engaging place to meet him. It lists scriptures, Sanskrit materials, images, and ritual implements, and includes his account of studying with Huiguo. Read the account of their meeting, then the sequence of initiations in the sixth, seventh, and eighth lunar months of 805. His gratitude towards his teacher gives the documentary detail a human voice.

The catalogue also helps you picture how learning travelled. Written teachings, painted mandalas, and objects for ritual were brought together for use in a new setting. Thomas Eijō Dreitlein’s study of Kūkai’s samaya commitments translates relevant passages and explains the commitments accompanying initiation.

Famen Temple and the sound of the texts

The underground chamber at Famen Temple in Shaanxi was excavated in 1987. Its treasures included reliquaries, gold and silver objects, glass, ceramics, and textiles; the major excavation report, Famensi kaogu fajue baogao, appeared in 2007. Esoteric Buddhist imagery on some reliquaries connects the finds with the mandala traditions encountered in these scriptures. The objects also illuminate the meeting of relic devotion, court patronage, and ritual artistry.

Sound provides another way into this world. Siddham is a script used to write Sanskrit, including mantra syllables. Chinese translations also preserve Indic sounds through Chinese characters, sometimes with notes on vowel length or joined consonants. When you reach such a passage, first identify where the mantra begins and ends. Then notice what comes immediately before it: a gesture, an invocation, or an instruction for contemplation. Gradually, the relationship between word, image, and action comes into focus.

Texts to read

  • Mahāvairocana Sutra 大日經

    An esoteric scripture on awakening, mantra, and maṇḍala practice, central to Tang Esoteric Buddhism and Shingon.

  • Sūtra of the Guiding Principle (Liqu jing) 大樂金剛不空真實三麼耶經

    An esoteric Prajñāpāramitā scripture on the purity of all things and the great bliss of Vajrasattva.

  • Humane Kings Sutra (Amoghavajra Version) 仁王護國般若波羅蜜多經

    The later Chinese version of the scripture on wisdom and protection of the state.

  • Vajraśekhara Sutra 金剛頂經

    An esoteric scripture on Vajradhātu maṇḍala practice and the realization of buddhahood.

  • Praise of Samantabhadra's Vows 普賢菩薩行願讚

    Verses expressing Samantabhadra's vows of bodhisattva conduct.

  • Cundī Dhāraṇī Sūtra, Spoken by the Mother of Seven Koṭis of Buddhas 七俱胝佛母所說准提陀羅尼經

    The dhāraṇī of Cundī, mother of seven koṭis of buddhas.

  • Mārīcī Sūtra 佛說摩利支天經

    Mārīcī and protective dhāraṇī recitation.

  • Catalogue of Imported Items 御請來目錄

    Kūkai's catalogue of texts, images, and ritual objects brought from Tang China to Japan.

Masters

  • Shanwuwei (Śubhakarasiṃha) 善無畏

    Śubhakarasiṃha (637–735) was an Indian monk who arrived in Chang'an in 716.

  • Jingangzhi (Vajrabodhi) 金剛智

    Vajrabodhi (671–741) was an Indian monk who travelled by sea through Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia before reaching China in 720.

  • Bukong (Amoghavajra) 不空

    Born in 705, Amoghavajra became Vajrabodhi's disciple while young and accompanied him to Tang China.

  • Huiguo 惠果

    Huiguo (746–805) was a disciple of Amoghavajra and an esoteric Buddhist teacher at Qinglong Monastery in Chang'an.

  • Kūkai 空海

    Kūkai (774–835), born in Sanuki, travelled to Tang China in 804.

  • Yixing 一行

    Yixing (683–727), born Zhang Sui, was a Tang monk, astronomer and mathematician.
